Skiing in Japan for the First Time: An Instructor's Checklist

Written by Schi, working ski instructor (SAJ Crown Prize)
Updated 2026-11-16
Ad: this page contains affiliate links.

Who this is for

I have taught first-timers every winter for years, most of them adults who have never stood on snow. This is the list of things I end up explaining in the first ten minutes of a lesson, written down once. It covers packing, renting, what a day costs, lessons, choosing a resort, the day itself, and safety.

What to pack

Japanese resorts rent almost everything. In YukiNavi, 312 of the 320 resorts with rental data have a rental shop, and several large ones (for example GALA Yuzawa Snow Resort, Karuizawa Prince Hotel Ski Resort, Furano Ski Resort, Ishiuchi Maruyama Snow Resort) rent jackets and pants as well. You still need to bring the small items, because rental shops often do not carry them.

  • Base layer top and bottom (synthetic or wool, not cotton) and a fleece mid layer.
  • Waterproof gloves. Thin liner gloves help on cold Hokkaido days.
  • Goggles and a neck gaiter. Sunglasses are not enough in falling snow.
  • One pair of thin ski socks. Thick socks make boots hurt.
  • Sunscreen and lip balm. UV at altitude is strong even in January.
  • Cash. Big resorts take cards; small municipal hills often do not.

Renting vs buying

My opinion as an instructor: rent skis and poles, buy boots first. A rental boot that is half a size wrong is the most common reason a beginner has a bad day, and it is the one piece of equipment that follows you to every resort. Skis matter far less at your level, and rental shops let you swap lengths.

If you ski once a year, rent everything. If you plan three or more days this season, buying boots pays off in comfort before it pays off in money.

What a day costs

Lift pass is リフト券 (rifuto-ken). Of the 403 resorts in YukiNavi, 355 list an adult one-day price. The median is ¥4,800. The middle half of resorts fall between ¥3,140 and ¥6,000. The cheapest listed is ¥300 (a town hill in Hokkaido) and the most expensive is ¥16,700 (Rusutsu Resort). The famous names are well above the median: Niseko United ¥13,500, Hakuba Happo-one ¥9,800, Nozawa Onsen ¥7,800.

Rental is the second cost. As one data point, Karuizawa Prince Hotel Ski Resort lists an adult ski set at ¥7,500 for the day, ¥7,200 if paid online in advance. Add a lesson, lunch, and the bus or train, and a first day at a major resort is roughly two to three times the lift pass. At a mid-size regional resort it is closer to the pass price doubled.

Whether to take a lesson

Take a half-day group lesson on day one. I say this knowing I am paid to teach, so here is the reason: the first two hours decide whether you learn to stop and turn or learn to fall. Friends who ski are usually bad teachers because they cannot remember how they learned. After a half day you know enough to practice alone on the beginner slope in the afternoon.

English-language lessons exist at 51 resorts in the data, mostly in Niseko, Hakuba, Yuzawa and the resorts run by Prince Hotels. Elsewhere, schools teach in Japanese. A beginner lesson is mostly demonstration and imitation, so it still works, but confirm the language when you book.

How to pick a resort

For a first trip, three things matter more than snow quality.

  1. Access. Shorter is better. Pick a resort you can reach by train and a short shuttle, so the day is not spent in transit. GALA Yuzawa Snow Resort is 71 minutes from Tokyo by shinkansen and the station opens into the resort.
  2. Beginner terrain. YukiNavi shows the beginner, intermediate and advanced share where the resort publishes it. Karuizawa Prince Hotel Ski Resort is 58 percent beginner. Hakuba Happo-one is 30 percent beginner and 50 percent intermediate, which is better for a second trip.
  3. English support. The app rates each resort low, medium or high. High means English-speaking staff and menus. Only 14 resorts are rated high.

Do not pick by reputation. Niseko and Hakuba are famous for powder and long runs, which you cannot use yet, and you pay for them.

The flow of a day

Most resorts run lifts from about 8:30 to 16:00, some later. Arrive before the lifts open. Rental shops queue by 9:00 on weekends, and fitting boots takes longer than you expect. Buy the lift pass online where the resort offers it; 154 resorts in the data have an online ticket page and several sell it slightly cheaper than the window.

Ski two hours, eat early (restaurants fill between 12:00 and 13:00), ski two more hours, and stop while you still have energy. After the lifts close, the onsen, a hot spring bath, is the normal end to the day in most ski towns. Bring a small towel; many onsen rent or sell them but not all.

Safety basics

  • Wear a helmet. Rental shops have them.
  • Stay inside the ropes. Off-piste rules vary by resort and in Hokkaido the forest holds tree wells you can fall into head first.
  • The person below you has right of way. Look uphill before you start.
  • Drink water. Cold air hides how much you sweat.
  • Most injuries happen on the last run. I have seen this every season. Legs are tired, the light is flat, and people take one more run because the pass is paid for. Decide in advance that the second-to-last run is the last one.

The Japanese piste color code

Japan does not use the North American green circle, blue square, black diamond system, nor the European blue, red, black. The standard here is:

ColorJapaneseLevel
Green初級 (shokyu)Beginner
Red中級 (chukyu)Intermediate
Black上級 (jokyu)Advanced

Red in Japan is roughly a North American blue. Grading is relative to the resort, so a green at Hakuba Happo-one can be steeper than a red at a small local hill. Read the trail map at the base, and ask the lift staff if you are unsure where a lift leads. Many lifts serve only red and black runs with no easy way down.
